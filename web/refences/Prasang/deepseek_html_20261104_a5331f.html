The Three Periods

Kabir's works are traditionally grouped into three phases of composition.

c. 1440–1470

Early Period

The formative years — simple, direct sakhis on devotion, the guru, and the search for the Divine.

84 works Mostly Dohas
c. 1470–1500

Middle Period

The mature voice — sharp polemical sabdas critiquing ritual, caste, and hypocrisy in both Hindu and Muslim society.

92 works Sabdas & Dohas
c. 1500–1518

Late Period

The culminating vision — longer ramainis and mystical poems of unity, surrender, and the unstruck sound.

The Arc of a Life

First Poem vs. Last Poem

How did Kabir's voice change from his earliest to his final surviving verse?

Earliest Surviving c. 1440s

Sakhi #1 — Guru Govind Dono

गुरु गोविन्द दोउ खड़े, काके लागूं पाय।
बलिहारी गुरु आपने, गोविन्द दियो बताय॥

"The Guru and God both stand before me — whose feet do I touch first? I bow to the Guru, who showed me God."

Doha Guru devotion

Style: Simple, traditional, humble — a classic guru-vandana (invocation).

Latest Surviving c. 1510s

Ramaini — Kahai Kabir Suno Bhai Sadho

कहै कबीर सुनो भाई साधो,
सहज समाधि लागी।

"Says Kabir, listen O seeker — the spontaneous meditation has begun. The unstruck sound resounds, and the self dissolves in the Beloved."

Ramaini Mystical union

Style: Intimate, realized, inward — a sage speaking from experience, not doctrine.

AI Literary Analysis

The arc from the first to the last poem mirrors Kabir's spiritual journey: from the outward gesture of reverence (bowing to the Guru) to the inward state of realization (the unstruck sound within). The early Kabir asks who to worship; the late Kabir has become the worship. This movement from doctrine to experience is the signature of his creative evolution.

Scholarship

Criticism Through Time

How scholars and critics have read Kabir's works across the centuries.

c. 1600 · Early Compilation

The Bijak is compiled by the Kabir Panth — the first major collection of his verses, organized into sakhis, sabdas, and ramainis.

1920s · Nationalist Revival

Rabindranath Tagore translates Kabir into English; Gandhi and the nationalist movement adopt Kabir as a symbol of Hindu-Muslim unity.

1960s–80s · Modern Scholarship

Scholars like Charlotte Vaudeville and Parasuram Chaturvedi produce the first critical editions, separating authentic Kabir from later attributions.

2000s–present · Digital & Global

Kabir enters world literature through new translations, comparative studies with Sufi and Christian mysticism, and digital archives like Prasang.
