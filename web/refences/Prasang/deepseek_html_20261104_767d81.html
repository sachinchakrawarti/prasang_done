What Was the Bhakti Movement?

The Bhakti movement was a devotional revolution that swept across the Indian subcontinent roughly between the 7th and 17th centuries CE. At its heart was a radical idea: that the Divine could be reached not through priestly mediation, Sanskrit scholarship, or elaborate ritual, but through pure, personal love and devotion (bhakti).

Beginning with the Tamil Alvars and Nayanars in South India (c. 7th–9th century), the movement spread north through the Virashaiva saints of Karnataka, the Varkari poets of Maharashtra, and the Nirguna and Saguna poets of North India. It crossed religious boundaries, influencing Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs alike.

Bhakti poets wrote in regional vernaculars — Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Tamil, Kannada, Punjabi — rather than Sanskrit. This democratized spiritual knowledge, making it accessible to farmers, weavers, and women who had been excluded from elite religious learning. Their songs (bhajans, abhangs, vachanas, dohas) were transmitted orally for centuries before being written down.

The movement produced some of South Asia's most beloved poetry — Kabir's piercing dohas, Mirabai's ecstatic bhajans, Tulsidas's epic Ramcharitmanas, and Surdas's Krishna lyrics. Its emphasis on love over ritual, equality over hierarchy, and experience over scholarship continues to resonate today.

Comparative

Parallel Traditions

The Bhakti movement echoed — and was echoed by — devotional revolutions across the world.

Bhakti & Sufism

c. 11th – 17th century · South Asia

Both movements emphasized love over law, experience over doctrine, and vernacular over classical language. Kabir and Rumi, though separated by language and religion, share a strikingly similar vocabulary of longing and surrender.

Bhakti

"ढाई आखर प्रेम का"

Kabir

Sufi

"You are the ocean in a drop."

Rumi

Bhakti & Christian Mysticism

c. 13th – 16th century · Europe

Medieval Christian mystics like Meister Eckhart and Julian of Norwich echoed Bhakti's emphasis on direct, unmediated experience of the Divine. Both traditions used bridal mysticism — the soul as lover longing for union.

Bhakti

"मैं तो प्रेम दीवानी"

Mirabai

Christian

"Love is a fruit in season at all times."

Mother Teresa
